You spend your days teaching our kids, protecting our communities, responding when things go wrong, caring for others, or serving our country.

So when you're finally ready to buy a home, Texas has a program designed to give a little help back to the people who spend their careers helping everyone else.

And no, unfortunately, the state isn't handing out free houses.

I checked.

But the Homes for Texas Heroes Home Loan Program may provide qualifying Texas homebuyers with valuable down payment assistance (DPA) that can make getting into a home much more achievable.

What Is the Homes for Texas Heroes Program?

The program is offered through the Texas State Affordable Housing Corporation (TSAHC) and is designed to help eligible Texas heroes purchase a home.

Depending on the loan and assistance option available, qualifying buyers may be able to receive down payment assistance that can help cover expenses such as:

- Your down payment
- Closing costs
- Prepaid expenses
- Other eligible costs associated with purchasing your home

Translation?

You may not need nearly as much cash sitting in your bank account as you thought you did.

And that can make a BIG difference.

Who Is Considered a Texas Hero?

The program isn't limited to police officers and firefighters.

Eligible professions can include:

- Public school classroom teachers
- Teacher aides
- School librarians
- School counselors
- School nurses
- Firefighters
- EMS personnel
- Peace officers
- Corrections officers
- County jailers
- Certain public security officers
- Veterans
- Active-duty military
- Certain nursing and allied-health faculty

Specific eligibility requirements apply, so your occupation, income, credit, loan type and other factors need to be reviewed by a participating lender.

Wait... Do I Have to Be a First-Time Homebuyer?

Here's where a lot of people get confused.

Down payment assistance itself is not necessarily limited to first-time homebuyers.

So if you've owned a home before, don't automatically assume you're out.

That's one of the biggest reasons I encourage buyers to actually speak with a knowledgeable lender before deciding they don't qualify.

I've seen plenty of people talk themselves out of buying a house before anyone ever ran the numbers.

Don't decline yourself before the lender gets a chance to approve you!

How Does the Down Payment Assistance Work?

TSAHC offers different assistance structures depending on the mortgage and program.

Some assistance may be structured as a grant, while other options may be a three-year deferred forgivable second lien.

That second phrase sounds approximately 847% scarier than it actually is.

A forgivable second lien can have 0% interest and no monthly payment, and it can be completely forgiven after the required period if the program conditions are satisfied.

However, if you sell, transfer or refinance too soon, repayment may be required.

That's why choosing the right assistance option matters just as much as qualifying for it.

What About Credit?

You don't need a credit score of 812, a trust fund and a rich uncle named Mortimer.

But you do have to qualify.

TSAHC's current published requirements include minimum credit-score standards that vary depending on the mortgage and DPA option. Income limits also apply and can vary by county and program.

That's why I don't like giving someone a generic:

"Yep, you'll qualify!"

We need to look at your actual situation.

The Bigger Question: What Would Buying Cost YOU?

This is where things get interesting.

A lot of renters assume:

"I can't buy because I don't have 20% down."

For many buyers, 20% down simply isn't required.

Others assume they need $20,000, $30,000 or $40,000 sitting around before they're even allowed to look at houses.

That isn't necessarily true either.

Between low-down-payment mortgage options, down payment assistance and available seller or builder incentives on certain properties, the amount needed to purchase a home can sometimes be dramatically different from what buyers expect.

So instead of asking:

"Can I afford to buy a house?"

I want you to ask:

"What would it actually take for ME to buy a house?"

Those are two very different questions.
